Original editorial · Research note · 10 November 2026

What would count as evidence of intelligence?

Before debating whether a system is intelligent, we should be able to say what observations would change our minds.

It is easy to call a computational system intelligent when it produces a convincing answer. It is harder to decide whether that answer reveals a durable capability, a lucky response, or the successful reproduction of something already present in its training or design.

That difference is worth taking seriously. If every impressive output becomes evidence of intelligence, the word gradually loses its experimental meaning. Yet the opposite position—that only human-like reasoning can qualify—risks excluding systems whose mechanisms differ substantially from our own.

There is a more productive path: state the capability we are looking for, design conditions under which it might fail, and measure what actually happens.

Competence is not the same as continuity

Consider a system that can answer a difficult question in isolation. Now give it a task that unfolds across many decisions. Introduce new information after it has committed to a plan. Remove a tool it relied upon. Change the reward structure. Return to the task after an interruption.

The first test measures a kind of competence. The later tests ask whether the system maintains an operational relationship with a changing world. Neither one alone settles the definition of intelligence, but together they reveal more than an isolated demonstration.

Persistent state is especially interesting here. Storing previous events is not the same as using them effectively. A system might retain an entire transcript and still repeat the same unsuccessful action. A meaningful test should ask whether prior outcomes alter subsequent choices—and whether those changes help under new conditions.

The environment is part of the experiment

Intelligence is often evaluated as though it were contained entirely inside a model. In practice, agent behavior depends on much more: available observations, tools, memory, constraints, feedback, and the cost of mistakes.

This does not mean that simply attaching a memory database or tool interface makes a system intelligent. It means that serious evaluation should document the whole operating arrangement. Otherwise, we may attribute a capability to a model that actually depends on scaffolding, or dismiss a controller because a narrow benchmark does not exercise what it was designed to do.

A transparent experiment describes the task environment as carefully as the system under test.

Five questions worth measuring

Adaptation. Does behavior change appropriately when conditions change? Compare performance before and after a controlled shift, not just on repeated familiar cases.

Retention. Does useful information survive across episodes? Test whether the system can apply a learned constraint after the original context is no longer visible.

Planning. Can the system choose actions for their delayed consequences? Compare it with a simpler reactive baseline and record the trade-off between planning cost and improved outcomes.

Recovery. What happens when a tool fails, a plan becomes invalid, or an action is rejected? Count successful recoveries, wasted steps and unsafe repetitions.

Transfer. Can a capability developed in one setting improve behavior in another without simply replaying a memorized solution?

None of these measurements is a universal intelligence test. Together, however, they produce evidence that can be challenged, replicated and compared.

A practical experimental protocol

Start with a small environment that has explicit states, actions and outcome measurements. Establish a baseline controller. Add one capability at a time—state retention, learned transition estimates, planning, or recovery logic—and run repeated trials under fixed and varying conditions.

Keep the task generator, random seeds, scoring rules and failure definitions available for inspection. Use held-out environment changes so that success cannot be explained entirely by repeated exposure to a fixed pattern. Report uncertainty across runs, not only a best demonstration. When a system fails, record the failure as carefully as the success.

Most importantly, distinguish what was observed from what was inferred. “The controller improved recovery after a tool outage in this benchmark” is an experimentally meaningful statement. “The controller understands failure” is a much larger interpretation that demands further evidence.

Why this matters

Research on computational intelligence will not become more rigorous by winning arguments over vocabulary. It will become more useful when it produces systems whose capabilities, weaknesses and operating conditions can be measured.

That standard is demanding, but it is also liberating. A small adaptive controller can be worth studying without being advertised as general intelligence. A large language model can demonstrate impressive competence without every impressive answer becoming proof of an enduring internal capability. Different architectures can be compared by the work they accomplish, the evidence they provide, and the conditions under which they break.

The question is not merely whether a machine appears intelligent. The stronger question is: what experiment would let us know more tomorrow than we know today?
