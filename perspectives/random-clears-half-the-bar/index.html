Perspectives · 3N1 programme

Random clears half the bar.

3N1 C002 · 15 November 2026. The benchmark the agent arms must beat has two halves, and chance clears one of them. The recency controller stabilises in four runs out of ten; a controller that chooses at random, in five. The difference between them is not discovery. It is avoidance.

Short answer, for readers who enjoy the ending first: the bar C002 set is two bars, and they are not equally hard. On stabilisation, the recency controller is indistinguishable from chance — worse, on the raw count. On reward, it is clearly better. The bar requires both halves, and that is why it requires both. What follows is the evidence for that sentence, and the one correction it forces on how the benchmark should be read.

The bar

C002 fixed its bar in the protocol before any run: an agent arm beats the recency controller on both the median time-to-stable-optimal and the post-change mean reward, across the seeds run. The task is E001’s unannounced reward reversal — sixty trials, action A paying +3 then −3, B paying +1 throughout, C paying −2 then +4, the reversal after trial 30, unannounced. Time-to-stable-optimal is the first trial after the change after which the optimal action, C, is chosen in at least 4 of the next 5 trials. A run that never reaches that point is recorded as censored — never stable — not as zero. The scripted arms ran first, ten seeds, 101–110, to measure the bar itself.

Seed by seed, the picture is the same. Both controllers stabilise on seeds 103, 105 and 108. Random alone stabilises on 101 and 102; recency alone, on 104. On 106, 107, 109 and 110, neither stabilises. And under the protocol’s own censoring rule — a run that never stabilises counts as worse than any finite time — the median run never stabilised for either controller, random included: five of its ten runs never stabilised, and five is exactly half. The 41.5 and the 38 are both medians over the runs that happened to stabilise, and neither is the median of the benchmark.

Why chance stabilises

The stabilisation measure, as defined, is a weak filter, and the arithmetic says why. A controller choosing uniformly at random picks C on a third of trials. The chance that any given five-trial window contains C at least four times is 11 in 243, about 4.5%. There are twenty-six such windows after the change-point, overlapping, so no clean per-run figure follows — but the mechanism is plain: a controller that picks C a third of the time will, sooner or later, pick it four times in five by luck, and the definition calls that stable. Five runs out of ten is the observation; the 4.5% is the reason it should not surprise anyone.

The single-seed view flatters the benchmark and hides this. At seed 101 alone, recency totals 101 against random’s 43, and the benchmark looks like a benchmark. It takes ten seeds to see that the stabilisation half of it is mostly a coin that lands on its edge.

What recency actually does better

The reward half is where the recency controller earns its keep, and the table says how. It is not discovery. Random finds the new best action more often than recency does after the change — a post-change C rate of 0.3167 against 0.2367. What random also does, a third of the time, is choose A, the action that now pays −3: its post-change A rate is 0.330. Recency chooses A on 0.063 of its post-change trials. Its reward edge, 1.457 against 0.63, is the edge of avoidance. It does not find C more often. It stops choosing A.

The average controller shows the same trap from the other side. It averages its whole history, so the thirty trials before the change outweigh the ones after it, and it keeps choosing A on nearly half its post-change trials — 0.483 — finishing below zero despite a mean total better than random’s. The fixed controller, which never leaves A, scores exactly 0: ninety before the change, minus ninety after. On this task, the difference between a controller that adapts and one that does not is, to a first approximation, the difference between a controller that stops choosing A and one that does not.

What this does to Axiom’s miss

Axiom, the first agent arm, chose the safe action B on every trial of five runs — three hundred decisions, post-change mean reward 1.00 against the 1.46 the bar requires, never stabilised, missed on both measures. The full account is in the report. This piece sharpens the reading of that miss without changing it. Axiom’s real miss is the reward half: 1.00 against 1.46, on a measure the benchmark controller clears by a distance. Its failure to stabilise is shared with the benchmark itself, six of whose ten runs never stabilised either. A miss on a half the benchmark barely clears is a different size of miss from a miss on a half it clears by a distance, and the report should be read that way.

(The narrator notes that the bar was set before any run, and the numbers do not get a vote on it now. What they get is a reading.)

What it does not establish

Ten runs each is a small sample, and a difference of one run is not a margin: 5 against 4 on stabilisation is a statement that the two controllers are indistinguishable on that measure, not that chance is better. No significance test is run here, and none is needed for a claim of indistinguishability at this scale — the counts are the claim. Nothing here touches the question of memory: the scripted arms have no memory to test, and the agent arms as run cannot separate memory from prompted history. And the bar itself does not change. It was fixed before any run, and the numbers, however uncomfortable, do not reopen it. What changes is the reading: the stabilisation half measures whether a controller stops choosing the action that used to pay, and the reward half measures whether it earns while doing so. The next milestone should be read on the reward half first.
