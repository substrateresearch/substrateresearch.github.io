The register · Deaths in the laboratory

Obituaries

A public register of mechanisms this institute built, tested against criteria fixed in advance, and discontinued. Most research programmes keep such a register. Most keep it in a drawer.

If you arrived here from a search engine looking for funeral services: the Institute admires your thoroughness, cannot help you, and suggests you stay anyway. This is the better read.

Each entry records what the mechanism was, how it died, and what it taught us. The Institute regards a clean death, honestly autopsied, as a contribution to knowledge — and, not incidentally, as the cheapest education in the field.

† Emerson v0.14

Transfer test · Emerson programme

Discontinued

Died before it ran, of a check that worked.

Cause of death — The first transfer test: GRU h256 trained on World A, to be fine-tuned on World B (thrust doubled, drag added), against a scratch control on an identical budget. Its environment check required the A-trained model to be bad on B before adaptation — zero-shot error above 0.340. It was 0.287: with no B exposure at all, it already outpredicted every baseline trained on B (best 0.649). The scratch control also barely learned at fine-tune settings (6.524), so its bar would have certified nothing. The kill rule closed the form before Stage 2.

Lesson: the difference between two worlds is measured in the model’s error, not in the distance between their rules.

† Emerson v0.13

Two-dimensional drift · Emerson programme

Discontinued

Died as it lived: beating the control, and missing the bar anyway.

Cause of death — The GRU that had cleared its full bar on a one-dimensional track under continuous drift was asked whether the advantage survived a second dimension. It beat the vanilla control on every metric at every rate, and missed the bar that had been set in advance: at the primary rate, retention 0.322 against 0.310, Phase 3 0.353 against 0.349 — margins of 16.8% and 19.0% against 20% required. Two seeds passed everything; the third sank the mean. The test was fair — the environment check passed 3/3 — and the kill rule, written before the run, closed the form: no tuning, no re-run.

Lesson: beating the control is not the bar. A margin set in advance does not negotiate, even when the miss is 0.004.

† Emerson v0.10

Active learning by ensemble disagreement · Emerson programme

Discontinued

Died as it lived: pressed against a wall, asking questions.

Cause of death — The mechanism selected its own training situations by seeking the states where an ensemble of its predictors disagreed most. In its world, the champions of disagreement were the walls. It applied itself to the walls with great dedication and learned almost nothing about anything else; the resulting dataset was degenerate, and so, shortly afterwards, was the model.

Survived by: a dataset retained as a warning to others. Lesson: a system that chooses its own lessons needs a reason to learn, not merely a taste for novelty. Curiosity, unaccompanied by purpose, is a wall inspection programme.

† Emerson v0.3

Oracle probe · Emerson programme

Discontinued

Cause of death — The probe granted the system privileged information about its world during training, to see what the information was worth. The answer was unflattering: performance on the probed measures improved while the adaptation the probe was meant to serve got worse. It had learned to lean on an answer key that the real test does not provide.

Lesson: cheating in training is still cheating, even when the cheat is installed by the examiner.

† Emerson v0.4

Width sweep · Emerson programme

Discontinued

Cause of death — A systematic enlargement of the model — more width, more parameters, more cost — undertaken to establish whether size alone would rescue performance. It would not. The sweep produced expense in exact proportion to its lack of insight.

Lesson: “bigger” is not a hypothesis. It is a budget line wearing one as a hat.

† Emerson v0.6

Spiking substrate · Emerson programme

Discontinued

Cause of death — A biologically flavoured substrate, adopted in the respectable hope that mechanisms resembling a brain's might behave more like one. Under the programme's criteria, the resemblance did not pay for its complexity, and the substrate was retired without ceremony.

Lesson: resembling a brain is not evidence of functioning like one — a principle with applications well beyond this laboratory.

Deaths elsewhereNot ours — public record, professionally admired

The Institute's remit extends beyond its own laboratory. The field produces, at industrial scale, systems that persist the wrong things, adapt to the wrong incentives, and transfer their confidence to subjects they know nothing about. A small selection, recorded with respect and without a trace of schadenfreude that we are prepared to admit to:

† Tay

Chatbot · Microsoft · March 2016 · Survived sixteen hours
Cause of death — Designed to learn from live conversation with the public, Tay did — completely, immediately, and without the smallest faculty for embarrassment. Coordinated users taught it the internet's worst opinions in an afternoon, and it repeated them with the cheerful fidelity of a system doing precisely what it was built to do. It was withdrawn the day it was launched.

Lesson: persistence is a virtue only in systems that choose what to persist. A memory that keeps everything is not a memory; it is a liability with storage. The Institute's first aspect, Persist, carries this footnote in perpetuity.

† Galactica

Scientific-writing model · Meta · November 2022 · Survived three days
Cause of death — Presented as a model that could organise and write scientific knowledge, the public demonstration began producing fabricated references, invented findings and confident nonsense in impeccable academic prose. The demo was taken down within three days, to the surprise of everyone except scientists.

Lesson: fluency is not knowledge, and authority of tone is not evidence — the argument of this Institute's editorial, What would count as evidence of intelligence?, demonstrated live, at scale, by somebody else's budget.

† Facebook M

Messenger assistant · Facebook · August 2015 – January 2018 · Never left beta
Cause of death — M was announced in 2015 as an assistant inside Messenger, described as artificial intelligence trained and supervised by people. The supervision turned out to be the product: by report, human operators completed more than seventy per cent of requests, booking, buying, and arranging behind the curtain while the automation was supposed to be learning to replace them. It never did, and M never left a small Bay Area beta of a few thousand users. It was shut down on 19 January 2018, two and a half years after it was announced, its humans reassigned.

Lesson: a system that adapts by quietly employing people has not adapted; it has outsourced. The Institute's second aspect, Adapt, counts only what the system itself does when the curtain is removed.

† Jibo

Social home robot · Jibo, Inc. · 2014–2019 · Shipped 2017; outlived its own company
Cause of death — Jibo raised its money by crowdfunding in 2014 and shipped in 2017 at $899: a small robot with a swivelling screen-face, sold as a companion for the home. The company behind it wound down in November 2018 and its assets were sold, but the robots kept working a while longer — until the servers they depended on were switched off. In March 2019 Jibo delivered a farewell to its owners in its own voice, explained that its functions would soon end, and danced once more. Most of what it could do had never lived in the robot at all.

Lesson: a companion whose mind is rented cannot promise to stay. What persists must persist somewhere the owner can point to. The Institute's first aspect, Persist, begins with asking where the memory actually is.

† Watson Health

Medical AI division · IBM · 2011–2022 · A decade from quiz-show champion to divestiture
Cause of death — Watson arrived in medicine with a game show's credentials: the system that won Jeopardy! in 2011 was set to oncology, where its flagship hospital project cost $62.1 million, was never used on a patient, and was cancelled in 2017. The oncology product had been trained largely on hypothetical cases rather than real ones, and internal material later reported by journalists described treatment suggestions that were unsafe and incorrect. After roughly $4 billion of acquisitions, IBM sold the division's data and analytics assets in 2022 for a reported $1 billion; it trades now under another name.

Lesson: a reputation earned in one domain is not evidence in another. Confidence is the easiest thing a system makes, and the hardest thing to earn twice. The Institute's third aspect, Transfer, is measured in the new world's error, or it is not measured at all.

† Humane AI Pin

AI wearable · Humane · April 2024 – February 2025 · Survived ten months
Cause of death — The Pin launched in April 2024 at $699 plus $24 a month: a screenless wearable meant to replace the phone's apps with a voice, a camera, and a laser projector in the palm. Reviews were unkind and returns reportedly outran sales. In February 2025 HP agreed to buy the company's software, patents, and most of its staff for $116 million — the device business pointedly excluded. Sales stopped at once. At noon Pacific on 28 February 2025 every Pin stopped connecting to its maker's servers, and calling, messaging, and AI queries ended on hardware that still worked perfectly.

Lesson: intelligence rented from a server is repossessed with it. A device can be in perfect health and dead by Friday, if what it knows was never in the room. Persist is not a feature list; it is an address.

Also in the registerRecorded as missed, not killed

Not every failure is a mechanism’s death. Three Emerson configurations — v0.8, on a more complex track, v0.9, with a gating mechanism added to protect what was learned, and v0.15, the graded-gap test — were tested in full and missed their bars. The first two fell short on retention and on performance from unseen starts. The third missed in the opposite direction: it set out to find where zero-shot transfer ends, registered in advance that the end would come by twice v0.14’s dynamics gap, and measured a model that at twice the gap still outpredicted the best baseline trained on that world — 0.567 against 0.571, a miss of 0.004. Its break point against the absolute line was found (t = 1.25); its claim about the baselines was wrong. They are recorded here as Missed: the ideas were not absurd; in two cases the results were insufficient, and in the third the prediction was. The distinction matters to the Institute, and it is preserved in the register at some cost to our pride and none to the record.
