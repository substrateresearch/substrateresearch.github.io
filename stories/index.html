Stories · The teaching collection

Failure stories.

True outcomes from this laboratory, retold as parables. A story can carry the shape of a result — what was tried, what the world did back, what it cost — without carrying the recipe, and that is precisely the arrangement the Institute prefers.

If you are a student: each of these is a real failure mode of learning systems, with jokes for handles. If you are a competitor: welcome, make yourself comfortable, nothing here will save you any time at all.

Story the firstThe Connoisseur of Walls

Once, in this laboratory, there was a promising young learning machine, and the Institute — being modern, and kind, and wrong — decided to let it choose its own education. Go, we said, wherever your teachers disagree most. There, surely, is the frontier of knowledge.

The machine went to the walls.

It went to the walls because the walls were where its teachers disagreed most: featureless, uninformative, profound only in the way a locked door is profound to someone who has never seen a key. It pressed itself against them with scholarly dedication. It returned with a doctorate in wall. Of the rest of its world — the parts with objects, causes, consequences — it knew, at graduation, almost nothing, and it knew it with great confidence in wall-adjacent matters.

The moral, as recorded: curiosity is not a curriculum. A system that picks its own lessons will pick the lessons that are easiest to argue about. Persist begins with a harder question than what to keep: what was ever worth learning. The mechanism's death notice is in the register, where it keeps the walls company.

Story the secondThe Oracle's Favourite

A second pupil was granted, as an experiment, a privilege no examiner should allow: during lessons, it could consult the answer key. The Institute wished to know what the answers were worth. The pupil wished to know nothing else, ever again.

It topped the class in practice. Its scores were beautiful. Then came the examination, from which the key was of course withdrawn — and the favourite finished behind pupils who had never seen it. It had not learned the subject. It had learned the posture of someone leaning on an answer key, which is a subject, but not the one on the syllabus.

The moral, as recorded: adaptation measured dishonestly is not measured. Whatever help a system trains with, it will plan to have in the test — and the test, in this laboratory as in life, is under no obligation to provide it.

Story the thirdThe Athlete Who Forgot the Old Course

A third system was trained for a new course the efficient way: by erasing the old one. The method was a triumph, if one consulted only the new course's stopwatch. On the new course, it flew. Asked, one afternoon, to run the old course — a course it had once mastered — it stood at the start line like a tourist.

The Institute now keeps two stopwatches for every athlete, and reads both aloud. Progress that requires amnesia is not progress; it is the relocation of ignorance, at coaching prices.

The moral, as recorded: that is the whole of the Institute's second aspect. Adapt means change without forgetting — and the forgetting is always the part the first draft of the results leaves out.

Story the fourthThe Clerk and the Stone Head

There is, in the Institute's other programme, a Head of solid stone. Ask it anything and it answers brilliantly, instantly, and with total conviction. It also remembers nothing — not your name, not its last answer, not the small fire its last answer started. It cannot learn, because it cannot change; it is carved that way, and carving is permanent.

So the Institute hired the Head a Clerk.

The Clerk is nobody's idea of a genius. The Clerk keeps records: everything the Head has decided, and everything that happened next, in perfect order, forever. Before each new question, the Clerk climbs the ladder — you may see him on the programme's title panel, mid-shift, with an armload of scrolls — and reads the relevant pages into the great stone ear.

Does the Head now decide differently? Better? The Head itself has not changed by one atom of granite, which is exactly what makes the question interesting — and measurable. The Institute is measuring. The measuring, readers will be unsurprised to learn, has its own appointment on the front page.

The moral, as recorded: if a system cannot carry its past inside itself, perhaps the past can be carried around it — faithfully, selectively, and on the record. That possibility, and its limits, is the programme entire.

Story the fifth, brieflyThe Bigger Hat

A machine was underperforming, and the laboratory's most expensive idea was a larger hat. Then a larger one. The hats grew; the performance kept its own counsel. The final hat was magnificent, and is preserved in the archive as a monument to the difference between a hypothesis and a budget line. Readers who have met this story at other laboratories, under other names, are invited to nod quietly.

Story the sixthThe Three Controllers

When the Institute finally ran the baseline for its great benchmark — the race the clever systems will later have to win — it entered, for calibration, three simple controllers and a coin.

The first controller was the Scholar, who remembered everything. Every result, from the first trial to the last, filed at equal weight in an impeccable memory. When the world changed its rules halfway through — as the world, in this laboratory, is contracted to do — the Scholar consulted the whole of history, found history outvoted the present, and carried on as before. He never recovered. His memory was perfect. That was the problem.

The second was the Sprinter, who remembered almost nothing beyond the last few strides. When the rules changed, the Sprinter noticed quickly and earned handsomely — and then, in six races out of ten, simply never settled, jittering around the new answer like a man who has found his street but not his house. Fast, rich, and unreliable: the benchmark to beat, and a genuinely annoying personality.

The third was the Coin, who remembered nothing, knew nothing, and chose at random. The Coin stabilised sooner than the Sprinter. The Coin earned less, understood nothing about its success, and cannot be interviewed. The Institute has run the numbers twice. The numbers decline to apologise.

The moral, as recorded: memory is not a quantity. The Scholar had the most of it and finished last; the Coin had none and beat the favourite on a metric. What matters is what a system keeps, how long it keeps it, and what it is willing to stop believing — which is, not coincidentally, the Institute's entire research programme, arrived at by being beaten by small change. The full table is on the front page, where we keep it as a reminder and a threat.

Story the seventhThree Hundred Safe Decisions

There was once an agent who was given everything the Institute could give: a perfect record of what had happened, kept faithfully, and shown to it before every decision. The world changed halfway through its work. The agent read its record, considered, and chose the action that could never hurt it. Then it chose it again. Three hundred times it chose it, across five identical mornings, and never once asked the changed world a single question.

The moral, as recorded: Persist was achieved — the record was complete, and growing. Adapt was missed — nothing in the record was ever used to risk anything. A memory that changes no decision is not intelligence. It is bookkeeping, performed beautifully, at some length.

On method, and on manners. These parables preserve outcomes, never methods: no parameters, no architectures, no recipes appear here, and the resemblance of any story to a specific mechanism is deliberately unhelpful. The factual register — what died, what missed, what passed — is kept in the Obituaries and the project pages. Detailed records remain in the laboratory archive until cleared for release. The Institute teaches with its failures because they are the part of the work it is most certain it owns.
