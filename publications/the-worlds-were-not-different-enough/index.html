Publications · Technical report — Emerson programme

The worlds were not different enough.

Emerson v0.14-transfer · 16 November 2026. The programme’s first transfer test never ran: its own environment check refused to certify the arena. What the check found on its way to refusing is the subject of this report.

Emerson’s framework names three things a substrate must do: persist, adapt, transfer. The first two have cleared bars — v0.5 retained, v0.12 adapted under drift. Transfer had never been tested. v0.14 was built to test it, and died before the test could run: its own environment check refused to certify the arena. The Institute publishes the death in full, because what the check found on its way to refusing is the most interesting measurement the programme has made this year — and because the distinction between that measurement and a result is exactly the distinction this Institute exists to keep.

The design: a change of world

World A is the programme’s standard one-dimensional track: thrust gain 0.4, velocity that persists until a wall or the clip. World B kept the track, the interface, the resets and the wall rule, and changed two dynamics parameters: thrust doubled (gain 0.8) and a drag of ×0.90 per step, so velocity in B decays instead of persisting. The probe was to be GRU h256 trained on A and fine-tuned on B under the standard 10-episode adaptation budget, against the same architecture from random initialisation given the identical fine-tune data. The bar, in the programme’s usual form: at least 20% below the scratch control on adapted and on unseen-start error, means across seeds 7–9.

Stage 1: three checks pass

Stage 1 ran first, as always: the anchor, the references, the control, and the four environment checks. The anchor was exact — GRU h256’s Phase 1 error on World A is 0.170 (seeds 0.169, 0.171, 0.169), the vanilla RNN control 0.255; the harness reproduces v0.5 to the third decimal. World B proved learnable: trained from scratch under the full protocol, the GRU reaches 0.191 on B, against 0.170 on A. The world was non-degenerate (wall hits 12.1% of steps, velocity saturation 4.7%). Three checks passed.

The fourth check: not bad, not close to bad

The check required the worlds to be genuinely different to the model: the A-trained GRU, shown B with no B training at all, had to be bad — zero-shot error above twice its A error, above 0.340. It was 0.287 (seeds 0.301, 0.287, 0.273). Not bad. Not close to bad. With no exposure to World B whatsoever, the model trained on A already outpredicts every baseline that was fully trained on B — persistence 1.015, tabular 0.649, feedforward MLP 0.670 — and sits within reach of the GRU that was trained on B outright (0.191). On unseen starts in B it scores 0.366, again ahead of every B-trained baseline.

The check was measuring the wrong difference. The preflight had verified that the two worlds’ rules disagree — on 65% of matched steps the next position differs by more than 0.1 — and the check assumed the model’s error would follow the rules’ disagreement. It did not, because most of what the model learned on A is not A’s dynamics in particular: it is how to infer a hidden velocity from a history of positions, how motion accumulates, what walls do. That structure is world-independent, and it carried. The difference between two worlds is measured in the model’s error, not in the distance between their equations. The Institute wrote the check; the Institute records the error as its own.

A second flaw, in the control

Stage 1 found a second flaw, in the control. The equal-budget scratch model — the same GRU from random initialisation, trained on the shared 20-episode fine-tune set at fine-tune settings (15 epochs, learning rate 1e-3) — reached 6.524, worse than predicting no change at all. Nothing in the programme had ever trained from scratch at a fine-tune learning rate, and at those settings the model barely learns. The bar computed from that control (adapted below 5.219) would have been passed by the zero-shot model without a single step of fine-tuning. A comparison against a control that cannot learn certifies nothing, and this one will not be asked to.

The kill rule closed the form

Per the spec’s kill rule (§5), the form is closed: no adjusting World B’s gain or drag, no enlarging the budget, no moving the check, no re-run. Stage 2 was not run and will not be; the probe script refuses on a failed environment check, and the Institute does not override its own refusals to manufacture a result.

What Stage 1 does not establish — and what it records

What Stage 1 establishes must be stated with its bounds. It does not establish that transfer passed a bar — no bar was ever legitimately in front of it. What it records is an observation, made by the baselines of a voided test: zero-shot transfer in this system is strong across this dynamics gap, strongest where the worlds differ least (error on coasting steps, where only the drag differs: 0.206; on thrust steps: 0.291 and 0.367) and incomplete where they differ most. Whether that carry survives larger gaps is a measurable question, and it is now the programme’s next test — a graded family of worlds, zero-shot error at each gap, a bar set in advance. v0.14’s contribution is to have found the question by failing to ask the previous one.

Ledger update

v0.14 enters the register as the sixth discontinued form in the Emerson programme: the Ledger’s count of mechanisms formally discontinued moves from 5 to 6, because unlike v0.11 — the previous death by environment check, which received no entry — this death produced a measurement worth citing. The count of configurations that have cleared a full bar stays at 2.

Records. v0.14-transfer specification (design and bar form approved before the run) and preflight; Stage 1 run records, seeds 7–9, run on CPU; the computed bar, recorded as never signed off and never in force, with the environment check failure recorded against it.
