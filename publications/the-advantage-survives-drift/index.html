The advantage survives drift.

Emerson v0.12-drift · 14 November 2026. The rule changed once, the model adapted, and it remembered the old rule afterwards. So the Institute arranged a harder appointment: a rule that never stops changing.

Short answer, for readers who enjoy the ending first: the advantage survives — at all three drift rates tested, on this track, with the qualifications stated below in type no smaller than this. The longer answer includes a void run, a formula that failed its own check, and a diagnostic that pointed in the opposite direction from the one expected. The Institute publishes those too. Especially those.

The question left open by the fixed rule

The gated recurrent model at the centre of the Emerson programme — a GRU (gated recurrent unit) with hidden width 256 — cleared its full bar once before, on a fixed rule with a single reversal. In v0.5 it recorded Phase 1 error 0.170, adapted 0.320, retention 0.300 and Phase 3 generalisation 0.447, with every seed under every part of the bar. It was the first mechanism in the programme to do so.

That left a narrower question open. The retention advantage — the one thing the gated cell did that no earlier mechanism managed — was measured against a rule that changed once and then settled. Real environments do not settle. Version 0.12-drift therefore asked whether the advantage survives a rule that never stops changing: a rule that drifts, continuously, for the whole episode.

In the Emerson testbed a small neural world model predicts how its environment changes from position alone — velocity is hidden, so the model must infer it from history. Drift was defined by a direction that follows a sine over time, at three rates: T=200 (slow), T=100 (medium, the primary rate) and T=50 (fast). The models themselves were unchanged from v0.5. The bar was fixed in the specification before any run: in Stage 1 the vanilla recurrent control sets the baselines, and in Stage 2 the GRU at hidden 256 must beat that control by at least 20% on retention and on Phase 3, and beat it at all on adapted error. If the bar failed, the specification’s kill rule closed this form of the experiment. The bar does not move after the numbers arrive.

The first attempt was void — and the check caught it

The first attempt to answer the question was void, and the Institute publishes that before it publishes anything else.

The specification defined drift as direction(t) = sin(2πt/T). The formula started the sine at t=0 inside each episode, so at T=100 and T=200 the direction never went negative. It rose from 0 to 1 and back. The rule became weaker, not different — and the specification’s own environment check caught it, on two of its three tests. Drift made prediction easier, not harder: the control’s error spike on drifting data was 0.231, against 0.255 on the fixed rule. The direction span was [0, 1] instead of the required [−0.9, 0.9]. Only T=50, which completes a full cycle per episode, actually reversed sign.

The test was declared void under the specification, and its numbers were preserved untouched — in a separate results record labelled as the void run — as a record of the defect rather than a result. A Stage 2 run carried out on that track at the time does not count, and nothing in what follows is built on it. The check is the check.

(A note from the narrator: institutes that never publish a void run are not institutes with better formulas. They are institutes with quieter filing systems.)

The fix changed the phase, not the bar

The fix was to centre the phase: direction(t) = sin(2π(t−25)/T), so the rule reverses sign mid-episode at every rate. It was recorded as Amendment 1 to the specification before any re-run. The amendment changed nothing else — same rates, same bar form, same 20% margin, same two-stage protocol, same models.

The corrected run passed its environment check on all three tests. Drift is now harder than the fixed rule: the control’s spike at T=100 is 0.425, against 0.255 on the fixed rule, and drift is harder at every rate. The direction span at T=100 is [−1.0, 0.998], inside the required range and beyond it. The fixed-rule anchor still reproduces v0.5 exactly, with a Phase 1 mean of 0.255. The controls passed as well: the fixed-rule path is unchanged by the drift code.

Only then was the bar computed, from the fresh Stage 1 baselines and by the pre-set formula. At the primary rate, T=100, the control recorded adapted 0.313, retention 0.337 and Phase 3 0.549. The bar for the GRU was therefore retention below 0.27, Phase 3 below 0.439, and adapted below 0.313. That bar was signed off before Stage 2 ran.

The corrected run passes at all three rates

Stage 2 ran 27 runs — three conditions, three rates, three seeds. At the primary rate the GRU at hidden 256 cleared every part of the bar, and cleared it cleanly: not only on the mean across seeds, but with every seed under every part of the bar. Seed 9’s retention, 0.267 against a bar value of 0.27, was the closest margin in the primary test.

What the result does not show

The caveats are part of the result, and they are stated here with the same prominence as the pass.

  • Scale. This is a one-dimensional toy, three seeds, and one mechanism. Nothing here is a claim about general intelligence, or about the GRU as anything other than the configuration that was tested.
  • One seed. At T=50, seed 8’s retention is 0.258, above the 0.25 bar value for that rate, although the mean of 0.232 passes. At the primary rate every seed passes; at the fast rate the pass is a mean pass, and the record says so.
  • The diagnostic went the other way. The specification expected error to be highest where the direction changes fastest, near a direction of 0. It is not. Error is highest where the direction is nearest −1 — most reversed from the fixed rule the model trained on. At T=100 the GRU’s error by drift phase is 0.169 where the direction is strongly positive, 0.169 in the middle range, and 0.434 where it is strongly negative; the control shows the same pattern at higher values. The adapted model struggles most where the rule is most different from what it learned, and a short fine-tune does not fully close that gap. That is a finding, not a footnote, and it is not a reason to re-run: the kill rules and the bar were settled in advance, and both have done their work.

What the result establishes is narrow, and the Institute will not widen it: the GRU’s retention advantage is not specific to a rule that changes once and settles. It survives continuous change, at all three rates tested, on this track.

Ledger update and the next experiment

This is the second configuration in the Emerson programme to clear a full bar, and the first to do so under continuous drift. The Ledger entry for “configurations that cleared a full bar” therefore moves from 1 to 2. The void run is entered in the Ledger as a void run; it is not deleted, and it is not counted as either a pass or a failure of the model.

What comes next is the harder environment. On the two-dimensional track the GRU still beat its control on every metric, but its margin shrank below the signed-off 20% bar — so the win did not reverse, and it did not clear the bar either. Drift on that two-dimensional track is the next experiment.

(The narrator notes that the Institute’s Ledger now contains two passes and a considerably longer list of everything else, and that the Institute considers this ratio a feature. Readers who prefer the other ratio are spoiled for choice elsewhere.)
