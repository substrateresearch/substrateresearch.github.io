We Built the Environment. Something Learned in It.

Beyond Human Discovery, Article 05 · 24 November 2026. What happened when the philosophy met an experiment: the November articles argued that intelligence is what a system can learn when its world changes; this essay reports what the record shows.

In November, this series argued a philosophy.

It argued that human-led discovery is reaching its limits, and that machines will take part in science whether we are ready or not. It argued that we measure machine intelligence against the wrong standard — that we are not measuring intelligence, we are measuring similarity to ourselves. It argued that intelligence is not what a system knows, but what it can learn when it encounters the unknown. And in a companion piece, it argued the claim everything else stands on: that intelligence is not a property of a model alone, but a relationship between a system and its environment.

Philosophy that never meets an experiment is just taste, stated confidently.

So we built the environment.

The instrument

Emerson is not a language model, and it is not large. It is a small recurrent world model — a few hundred thousand parameters — placed in a simple world and required to do one thing: predict what happens next, and act on its predictions. The world it lives in has a rule. The rule governs how things move, and the model never sees the rule. It sees only the world, one moment at a time, and it must infer everything else from what its actions reveal.

Then, partway through its life, we change the rule.

The smallness is deliberate, and it is worth defending, because the current instinct of the field runs the other way. A system with a few hundred thousand parameters can be understood in a way a system with a trillion cannot: when it fails, we can find out why; when it succeeds, we can check that it succeeded for the reasons we think it did. Scale would have hidden the very things this programme exists to see. Emerson is also never tested alone. Every result is read against baselines — random behaviour, a simple feedforward model with no memory, a tabular learner — because a number without a comparison is a rumor. Several of our early designs failed exactly here: approaches that sounded like progress — oracle hints, wider networks, explicit memory bolted on, feedback loops, active data collection — were built, tested against their bars, and killed when they did not clear them. The record of those failures is published too. A programme that only reports its survivors is not doing science; it is doing marketing with better vocabulary.

This is the whole experimental idea, and it comes straight from Article 03 of this series. If intelligence is the capacity to learn from the unknown, then the honest test is not a benchmark of what a system already knows. The honest test is to move its world and watch two things: what it retains of the old world, and how it changes for the new one. Before any run, we write down the bars — the numbers that will count as success — and the kill rules that will count as failure. The bars do not move after we see the results. That discipline is not decoration. It is the difference between an experiment and a story we tell ourselves afterwards.

What remained, and what changed

In the drift experiment, the model learned its world, the rule changed, and the model adapted to the new rule. Then we returned it to the original world and measured what was left.

Retention after drift scored 0.236, against a bar set in advance at below 0.270. Performance in the adapted world scored 0.259, against a bar of below 0.313. Both bars were cleared — not approximately, and not by adjusting the bars, which were frozen before the run began.

Read those two numbers together, because together they are the definition this work began with, no longer stated as philosophy but as measurement. Something remained: the original learning survived the change of worlds. Something changed: the system genuinely adapted to the new rule rather than merely persisting in the old one. Retention without adaptation is rigidity. Adaptation without retention is amnesia. Intelligence, if the word is to mean anything testable, lives in the tension between the two — and for the first time in this programme, both sides of the tension were present, in a system we built, under bars we set before we knew the answer.

The failure, at the same size

A bridge that only carries good news is not a bridge. It is a brochure. So here is the second result, in the same size type.

We built a harder world, in which a property that governs the world's behaviour is never directly observed. To succeed, the model had to infer the hidden property from interaction alone — to work out something true about its world that its senses never showed it — and carry that inference in memory.

It did. Before the relevant experience, its predictions sat at 0.4296; after, at 0.1716 — a 60 percent improvement, the first time in this programme that a system demonstrably identified an unobserved property of its world from interaction and used it. Article 02 warned against measuring intelligence by resemblance to ourselves. Nothing about this resembles us. It is a small machine, in a small world, knowing something it was never shown. It counts anyway.

And then it failed. Retention in that harder world scored 0.3363 against a bar of 0.295 or below. The system could infer, and it could adapt — but what it learned did not survive as well as the standard we had set in advance demanded. The bar was not lowered. The result stands in the record as a failure, next to the passes, in the same font.

This is not an embarrassment to be managed. It is the most informative result in the programme so far, because it locates the frontier precisely: inference is not the bottleneck we assumed. Retention under complexity is. The next work follows from that, and it follows from it only because the failure was recorded as plainly as the success.

There is a temptation, at exactly this point in any research programme, to quietly adjust the standard — to decide that 0.3363 is close enough, that the bar was conservative, that the spirit of the thing was met. Every reader who has worked in a laboratory or a company has watched this happen, usually in a meeting, usually with good intentions. It is the moment a research programme becomes a public-relations exercise, and it is invisible from outside, which is precisely why the bars are published before the runs. A standard you can move after the fact is not a standard. It is a mirror.

Accumulation is not memory

The companion evidence comes from memory research run alongside Emerson, and it is worth stating because it corrects an intuition nearly everyone in this field currently holds.

We compared ways of using a stored record: a cumulative store that keeps everything, a recency-only approach that keeps what is recent, and a richer hybrid designed to combine the virtues of both. The cumulative store — the biggest memory, the one that forgot nothing — was not the best performer. It was actively harmful, far worse than either alternative. And the hybrid, for all its additional structure, matched plain recency almost exactly: a difference of 0.0023.

Keeping everything is not remembering. A larger transcript mostly adds interference. What matters is not the size of the store but the discipline of what is kept, what is allowed to fade, and what can be retrieved when the world has moved on. The November articles suspected this. The record now says it, with numbers.

What the philosophy missed

The five earlier articles got two things right: the environment is a teacher, not a backdrop; and the test of intelligence is the unknown, not the archive. But they underweighted a third thing, and the experiments exposed it.

A system that learns must also be able to show its work.

Everything reported in this article is checkable by a stranger: the bars were set in advance, the failures are published beside the passes, and the records can be re-run and verified without trusting the author. That is not a courtesy. It is the load-bearing wall. The work that grew alongside Emerson — on execution receipts, on whether an independent party can establish what a system was instructed to do, what it actually did, and whether the result satisfied the original contract — exists because Article 04's dream has a precondition. Before machines can be trusted as investigators, their investigations must be auditable. A discovery no one can verify is not a discovery. It is a claim.

The kind of scientists they will become

Article 04 of this series closed with a question: not whether machines will become experimental scientists, but what kind of scientists they will become.

The bridge suggests an answer. They will become the kind we can check — if we build the environments, the bars, and the records that make checking possible. We built a small environment, and something learned in it: something that retained, something that adapted, something that inferred what it could not see, and something that failed, in writing, at a bar it could not move.

Intelligence is what remains — and what changes — after the environment moves.

We know, because we moved it, and kept the record.
